# Guided discovery and relevant research

## Ask about product choices, supply professional judgment

Begin with what is already known: target audience, visitor task, approved value
proposition, brand constraints and available content. Ask the next question that
will change a meaningful decision. Offer a recommendation that follows from the
product, with room to disagree.

Examples to adapt, not a questionnaire:

- For a reporting tool: “Are visitors mainly practitioners who want to see the
  workflow, or managers comparing outcomes? I would lead with a real report and
  the time-saving workflow if practitioners are the main audience.”
- For a creator membership: “Would an editorial, content-first page or a more
  personal, creator-led presentation fit your audience? An editorial approach
  would let visitors sample the material before choosing a plan.”
- For an existing brand: “Which parts of your current identity should carry
  through? If you have a reference site, what appeals to you: its typography,
  composition, imagery or tone?”
- For a vague request for something modern: “Should this feel restrained and
  precise, or more expressive and energetic? Given the professional audience, I
  suggest restrained typography with a distinctive product demonstration.”

These are guided product conversations, not unbiased research instruments. During
actual user testing, avoid suggesting the answer; observe behavior and ask neutral
follow-ups. Separate what the owner prefers from what customers demonstrably need.

Do not ask routine questions about security, privacy hygiene, accessibility,
responsive layout, SEO fundamentals, performance, libraries or framework mechanics.
Apply suitable engineering baselines. A genuine business decision, such as which
customer roles the product serves or whether its content is private, is different
from asking the user to select basic engineering quality.

## References and constraints

For reference sites, identify the particular useful qualities rather than treating
the whole site as a template. An anti-reference can reveal what to avoid: repeated
feature cards, excessive motion, dense menus, impersonal stock images or a tone that
does not suit the product. Respect supplied branding and authorized assets; a link
is not permission to copy distinctive branding or proprietary content.

Prefer concrete content knowledge: real product screens, work samples, supplied
photography, useful examples and substantiated claims. Missing testimonials or
customer logos are not blanks to fill with invented endorsements. Mark illustrative
data as illustrative and use it only where it serves the requested product.

If preferences remain open and the user wants the agent to decide, make a reasoned
choice and expose the assumption. Do not block progress on fonts or color swatches.

## Research that informs a decision

Use current primary evidence proportionate to the uncertainty. For the relevant
market, examine public competitor positioning, navigation, feature presentation
and pricing. Record source/date and comparable terms; do not mistake a visible
pattern for proof that it works or compare incomparable billing units.

Research requested features and difficult interactions before proposing them.
When implementation suitability matters, consult official documentation for
maintained libraries compatible with the existing framework/runtime, licensing,
accessibility and actual feature needs. Reusing a compatible dependency can be
preferable to another library. This research does not authorize package upgrades,
scope expansion or a replacement stack. Explain optional alternatives and their
tradeoffs; retain accepted choices unless the user changes them.

Use evidence to answer a question, not to generate an exhaustive market report on
every turn. Separate observations, inferred opportunities, recommendations and
unresolved issues. Do not claim research that was not performed.

## Compact handoff inside existing artifacts

Include only details relevant to the product:

- Audience, visitor context, main job and trust/understanding barriers.
- Page/navigation journey, primary action, public pricing and purchase/auth order.
- Required brand assets, references and specific preferences or exclusions.
- Proposed visual direction, rationale and content actually available.
- Assumptions and meaningful open product choices, with local acceptance cases.

For a monetised public product, default to public pricing with signup before
purchase. Do not add billing when monetisation has been rejected. A private
product, sales-led journey or pay-before-signup flow requires the corresponding
accepted product decision; do not infer it from a visual reference.

## Evidence behind the method

[GOV.UK user-needs guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/start-by-learning-user-needs)
supports starting from users, tasks, context and evidence, distinguishing
assumptions from observations. It is service-design guidance, not evidence for a
particular commercial visual style.

[Design Council's framework](https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/resources/framework-for-innovation/)
supports exploring a problem and candidate solutions before commitment. Use that
principle inside the existing App Builder stages; it does not mandate new stages.

[NN/g's B2B pricing research](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-price/) reports
buyers leaving sites that obscure prices. This supports discoverable pricing, but
the older qualitative findings do not provide a current conversion estimate.
