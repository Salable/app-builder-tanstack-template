# From preferences to a useful visual direction

Translate the product's audience, content and accepted preferences into a coherent
page composition. “Clean” or “modern” is not enough to distinguish two designs.

When useful, discuss a few descriptive contrasts with examples: restrained versus
expressive, editorial versus tool-like, spacious versus information-dense,
photographic versus illustrative, formal versus conversational. Do not turn these
into a mandatory questionnaire or a numerical brand score. A spacious marketing
page and a dense working interface can serve the same product appropriately.

A direction should explain its information hierarchy, layout/grid, reading rhythm,
typography, imagery or product demonstration, tone and interaction. Color, shape
and decoration support that composition. Do not offer the same page three times
with different palettes and call them different directions.

If a choice would help, present two or three concise alternatives with a reason
each fits the same brief. A small visual sample and representative page/section
sketch can make the differences understandable without producing full competing
sites. Use visual tools only when available and useful. If the user has delegated
the decision or already supplied a clear direction, choose or follow it.

Illustrative families to investigate, not fixed sector templates:

| Product context            | Possible emphasis to evaluate                                                |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Developer/API product      | Working examples, integration path, documentation and usage/cost explanation |
| B2B workflow product       | Actual work sequence, role-specific outcomes and product evidence            |
| Membership/content product | Content previews, editorial hierarchy and access value                       |
| Marketplace                | Discovery, comparison and distinct participant journeys                      |
| Creator/service business   | Actual work, relevant case details and a clear enquiry or purchase path      |

Choose based on the actual audience and task. Do not assume developers need dark
terminal styling, business buyers need identical feature cards or every product
needs a three-plan table. Familiar controls remain useful; novelty is not an
acceptance criterion. Do not compare with another customer's private project or
require an arbitrary number of stylistic differences.

The owner may select an overall direction based on preference. That exploratory
choice does not establish the causal effect of each design element. If conducting
a controlled comparison later, isolate the relevant changes, account for ordering
effects and measure the intended outcome rather than simply asking which is nicer.

## Sources and limits

[Samantha Warren's original Style Tiles account](https://alistapart.com/article/style-tiles-and-how-they-work/)
describes translating stakeholder language into small visual samples. It is a
practitioner method for discussion, not experimental proof of higher conversion.
Combining a sample with a composition sketch is our recommendation to make layout
choices explicit.

[NN/g's visual-design testing guide](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/testing-visual-design/)
distinguishes aesthetic/brand feedback from observed task success. Its preference
testing guidance calls for perceptible differences and warns about ordering and
overly similar variants. A two- or three-direction exploration is a useful option,
not a scientifically optimal number or mandatory App Builder stage.
